  <section class="sciren-intro" aria-labelledby="sciren-about">
    <div>
      <p class="sciren-eyebrow">Bringing research into the classroom</p>
      <h2 id="sciren-about">Science students can explore</h2>
      <p>Our lab participates in the Scientific Research and Education Network (SciREN), a program that connects scientists with K–12 educators to bring current science into classrooms. This lesson draws on our research in animal physiology to help students explore how animals survive in different environments.</p>
      <p>Developed by <strong>David Adams, Isabella Burger, and Ellen Keaveny</strong>, the lesson combines animal examples, a hands-on investigation, and creative problem-solving.</p>
    </div>
    <aside class="sciren-facts" aria-label="Lesson at a glance">
      <h3>At a glance</h3>
      <dl>
        <div><dt>Audience</dt><dd>Middle school biology</dd></div>
        <div><dt>Time</dt><dd>Approximately 60 minutes</dd></div>
        <div><dt>Setting</dt><dd>Classroom, with options for learning at home</dd></div>
        <div><dt>Materials</dt><dd>Containers, water, food coloring, paper towels, construction or printer paper, timers, and notebooks</dd></div>
      </dl>
    </aside>
  </section>

  <section class="sciren-section" aria-labelledby="sciren-responses">
    <p class="sciren-eyebrow">Three ways animals respond</p>
    <h2 id="sciren-responses">From immediate actions to changes across generations</h2>
    <div class="sciren-concepts">
      <article class="sciren-concept">
        <span class="sciren-number" aria-hidden="true">01</span>
        <h3>Behavior</h3>
        <p class="sciren-example">Lizards in sun and shade</p>
        <p>A lizard can bask to warm up or move into shade to cool down. Students consider how changing location changes the conditions an animal experiences.</p>
        <p class="sciren-takeaway">An animal can respond by changing what it does.</p>
      </article>
      <article class="sciren-concept">
        <span class="sciren-number" aria-hidden="true">02</span>
        <h3>Physiology</h3>
        <p class="sciren-example">Salamander skin and water balance</p>
        <p>Salamanders can adjust properties of their skin that influence water loss. Students use different papers as a simple model of barriers with different permeability.</p>
        <p class="sciren-takeaway">Physiological plasticity occurs within an individual's lifetime.</p>
      </article>
      <article class="sciren-concept">
        <span class="sciren-number" aria-hidden="true">03</span>
        <h3>Evolution</h3>
        <p class="sciren-example">Brown bears and polar bears</p>
        <p>Students compare closely related bears and discuss how differences in fur, coloration, and insulation relate to life in different environments.</p>
        <p class="sciren-takeaway">Evolutionary change occurs in populations across generations.</p>
      </article>
    </div>
  </section>

  <section class="sciren-activity sciren-section" aria-labelledby="sciren-investigation">
    <div class="sciren-activity-heading">
      <p class="sciren-eyebrow">Hands-on investigation</p>
      <h2 id="sciren-investigation">Modeling salamander skin</h2>
      <p>Salamander skin is an important interface between an animal and its environment. In this activity, students investigate how different materials affect the movement of colored water.</p>
    </div>
    <ol class="sciren-steps">
      <li><strong>Predict.</strong> Working in pairs, compare a paper towel with construction or printer paper. Predict which will allow food coloring to pass through more quickly.</li>
      <li><strong>Set up.</strong> Fill two similar containers with water. Gently place a paper towel on the water in one and construction or printer paper in the other.</li>
      <li><strong>Observe.</strong> Add food coloring to each paper. Record how long it takes to pass through, where the color spreads, and how quickly it moves. Keep the amount of water, paper size, and number of drops consistent.</li>
      <li><strong>Interpret.</strong> Compare observations with predictions. Discuss how differences between materials can help illustrate the role of skin as a barrier.</li>
    </ol>
    <p class="sciren-note"><strong>Connecting the model to biology.</strong> This activity illustrates differences in permeability. It does not directly measure evaporation or salamander water loss. Use the comparison to discuss how skin properties can influence water movement in living animals.</p>
  </section>

  <section class="sciren-section sciren-extend" aria-labelledby="sciren-apply">
    <div>
      <p class="sciren-eyebrow">Make connections</p>
      <h2 id="sciren-apply">Design an animal for its environment</h2>
      <p>Students choose an environment, such as a desert, rainforest, grassland, or tundra, and design an imaginary animal with traits suited to living there. They explain how its behavior, physiology, and inherited traits could help it survive.</p>
      <p>Then change the conditions. If the habitat becomes warmer or drier, which responses could help immediately? Which could occur within a lifetime? Which would require evolutionary change across generations?</p>
    </div>
    <aside class="sciren-discussion">
      <h3>Questions to explore</h3>
      <ul>
        <li>What differs between brown bears and polar bears, and how might those differences help them in their habitats?</li>
        <li>How does moving into shade differ from changing skin properties?</li>
        <li>Why does evolutionary adaptation require generations rather than an animal deciding to change?</li>
      </ul>
    </aside>
  </section>
